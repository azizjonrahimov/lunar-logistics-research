# 04 — Demand, supply and competitors for a lunar surface logistics marketplace

Compiled 2026-10-05. All figures are sourced in the Reference list (section F); bracketed numbers like [R12] point to it. Items marked **UNVERIFIED** or **ANALYST ASSUMPTION** could not be confirmed from a primary source during this pass and should be re-checked before publication.

Context that reshaped this note: on 24 March 2026 NASA's "Ignition" event restructured Artemis into a 10-year, three-phase "Moon Base" plan; Artemis III became a 2027 LEO docking test, Artemis IV (early 2028) is now the first crewed landing, and Artemis V is late 2028 [R6][R7][R10]. On 26 May 2026 NASA redesignated three CLPS flights as "Moon Base I–III" and awarded Phase-1 LTV task orders to Astrolab and Lunar Outpost, with Blue Origin's Blue Moon MK1 as the delivery lander [R5][R6]. On 28 May 2026 a New Glenn was destroyed in a pad hot-fire, pushing Blue Moon MK1 Pathfinder to NET Q1 2027 [R14][R15].

## D. Existing or proposed marketplace-like services

**NASA CLPS.** IDIQ vendor pool: 9 companies (Nov 2018) + 5 (Nov 2019) = 14; cumulative maximum $2.6B through 28 Nov 2028; each task order buys end-to-end delivery "including payload integration, mission operations, launch from Earth, and landing" [R58]. NASA OIG (June 2024): 11 task orders to 5 vendors as of Feb 2024, $208.2M of cost growth, average delay ≥14 months, average 44 months from award to launch [R59]. Known task-order prices: IM-4 $116.9M (6 payloads); IM-5 $180.4M (7 payloads, Nova-D); Blue Origin SCALPSS carriage $6.1M [R14][R34][R35]. CLPS is a buyer-side procurement vehicle with one customer (NASA) — not a two-sided marketplace.

**ispace.** Published business model (Sept 2023 Mission 3 update deck): "Assumed payload unit price as of September 2023 is approx. $1.5MM/kg, and the Company assumes that the price will decrease over time"; prepaid payload service agreements collected between signing (1–3 years pre-launch) and launch; M3 sellable payload 50 kg to NASA for $55M, growing to ~160–168 kg sellable per mission by M8–M10 [R24]. Rover-payload rate $3.5M/kg and the Starship capacity resale ("Lunar Access Integrator", 500 kg for $50M) come from secondary sources [R53] — **UNVERIFIED**.

**Astrolab FLEX Services.** Offers payload hosting on FLEX ("FLEX Universal Payload is your payload platform, FLEX Rover is your delivery system"); seven listed partners (NASA, HPE, Argo Space, Astroport, Avalon Space, Interstellar Lab, LifeShip); no price list [R37]. $160M in contracts for ~1,000 kg of payloads was announced 21 Nov 2023 (implies ~$160k/kg, **analyst inference**) [R38].

**Lunar Outpost.** Eagle page has a "Book Payload" call to action, no pricing [R52].

**Blue Origin.** $234M per LTV delivery on a 3 t lander implies ~$78k/kg if fully manifested (**analyst inference**) [R6].

**Marketplace/platform startups found:** none operating a two-sided lunar *surface* logistics exchange. Adjacent concepts: Cislunar Space Development Company (reusable tugs LEO↔EML1↔surface, concept); Lunar Logistics Services (European payload broker onto Astrobotic flights); MOONPORT (academic transport concept); Lunar Cargo (Greece, 2021, net-capture concept); CisLunar Industries (in-space materials) [R60]. ispace's "Lunar Access Integrator" is the closest thing to a capacity aggregator, and it is a reseller, not an open marketplace. Searches for "lunar logistics marketplace", "cislunar logistics platform" and "Moon cargo booking platform" returned no operating company. Conclusion: a surface-mobility/offloading marketplace does not exist today; the gap NASA itself identifies (500–12,000 kg delivery and 1,000–15,000 kg relocation) is unserved by any platform.

## F. Policy constraints shaping a marketplace (one paragraph each)

**Artemis Accords, Section 11 (Deconfliction of Space Activities).** The Accords define a safety zone as "the area in which nominal operations of a relevant activity or an anomalous event could reasonably cause harmful interference," require that its "size and scope … reflect the nature of the operations being conducted and the environment," be "determined in a reasonable manner leveraging commonly accepted scientific and engineering principles," "change over time reflecting the status of the relevant operation," and be "temporary, ending when the relevant operation ceases"; signatories "should promptly notify each other as well as the Secretary-General of the United Nations of the establishment, alteration, or end of any safety zone," "commit to respect reasonable safety zones … including by providing prior notification to and coordinating with each other before conducting operations in a safety zone," and "commit to respect the principle of free access to all areas of celestial bodies" (§11 ¶7(a)–(d), ¶10, ¶11). 76 nations had signed as of NASA's page dated 25 Sep 2026. Implication: a marketplace's dispatch layer must ingest and publish safety-zone geometry and notifications, since every lander descent (>1,000 m ejecta standoff per NASA) and every traverse near another operator's assets triggers coordination duties. Text: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Artemis-Accords-signed-13Oct2020.pdf ; overview: https://www.nasa.gov/artemis-accords/ [R61][R62].

**Export controls (ITAR/EAR) for rover software.** Planetary rovers were moved from USML Category XV to the Commerce Control List on 10 Nov 2014 and are generally classified ECCN 9A515; related software "specially designed" for development, production, operation or maintenance of such spacecraft falls under 9D515, and 9A515 items "remain subject to the EAR even if exported … with ITAR-controlled items integrated." In October 2024 Commerce proposed a new License Exception for Commercial Space Activities (CSA) covering space-agency programs and research. Items that remain ITAR (e.g., certain sensors, autonomous navigation using classified-grade components) would bar non-U.S. operators from receiving tele-operation software or technical data without a license. A marketplace that brokers vehicle time to foreign payload owners must therefore segregate control software and tech data (deemed-export risk) from the commercial API layer. https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/11/new-export-control-rules-present-key-regulatory-changes-for-space ; https://www.wiley.law/alert-3161 [R63][R64].

**NextSTEP-3 Appendix B (Moon Base Demonstrations) eligibility.** NASA's page lists solicitation M2M-MSFC-0001 (released July 2026; white papers due 24 Aug 2026 after extension) covering Infrastructure, Power, Comms/PNT, Transportation, Mobility, Habitation, Autonomy & Robotics, Lunar Science and ConOps; the NextSTEP-3 Omnibus runs through 26 Sep 2029 and defers eligibility to each appendix. Secondary summaries state Appendix B "is open to U.S. institutions (companies, universities, nonprofits) only" and that NASA civil servants, JPL, national labs and FFRDCs may not prime. **The specific "U.S.-owned/controlled" ownership clause could not be verified** from the SAM.gov text in this pass (SAM.gov blocked fetches) — treat as UNVERIFIED and pull the PDF from https://sam.gov/workspace/contract/opp/4c48e9f1405941bf96f73c65afe03779/view. Implication: a marketplace that wants NASA demonstration funding must be a U.S. entity, and foreign vehicle suppliers (Venturi Space, Toyota/JAXA, ispace Japan) can only participate as subcontractors or via their U.S. subsidiaries. https://www.nasa.gov/general/nextstep-3-b-moon-base-demonstrations/ [R65][R66].

**Planetary protection for polar missions (NPR 8715.24 / former NID 8715.128).** NPR 8715.24, "Planetary Protection Provisions for Robotic Extraterrestrial Missions" (effective 24 Sep 2021), governs; it states that "All missions to the Moon and beyond are assigned a categorization for the outbound phase" and controls "organic materials, and volatiles carried or released by spacecraft." Under the lunar scheme first set by NID 8715.128 (July 2020), Category IIa (lunar surface outside PSRs/poles) requires reporting of propulsion volatiles, while Category IIb (PSRs and the lunar poles) requires both volatiles and a spacecraft organic inventory, "with no limits on the types or quantities of organics," reported pre-launch and at end of mission; NASA's April 2025 COSPAR update notes CLPS "commercial engagement provided feedback resulting in an update to the pre-launch organics inventory reporting form and streamlined a post-launch / end of mission reporting form for Category II lunar missions." The latitude bounds often cited for IIb (poleward of 79°S and 86°N) are from the NID as reported and were **not verified verbatim** (Appendix C of the NPR is an image table). Implication: every vehicle and cargo item a marketplace moves into a PSR needs an organics inventory record — a natural data field for a manifest system. https://nodis3.gsfc.nasa.gov/displayDir.cfm?t=NPR&c=8715&s=24 ; https://sma.nasa.gov/news/articles/newsitem/2020/07/20/nasa-updates-planetary-protection-policies ; https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250003523/downloads/COSPAR%20PPP%20-%20NASA%20Update%20-%20April%202025.pdf [R67][R68][R69].
