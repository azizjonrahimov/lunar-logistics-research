Who Hauls the Habitat? A Verified Simulation Testbed, a Pooled Dispatch Marketplace and a Reference Design for Lunar Surface Logistics

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, version 2, October 2026 · Code, data, seeds and figure scripts: github.com/azizjonrahimov/lunar-logistics-research · Live simulator: azizjonrahimov.github.io/lunar-logistics-research

Abstract. NASA's Moon Base plan lands about 4 tonnes of cargo at the lunar south pole before 2029, roughly 60 tonnes in 2029–2032 and 150 tonnes after that. Individual items range from 10 kg instruments to 15-tonne habitats, yet the rovers under contract carry 800–1,600 kg, and NASA's own architecture papers say that no planned vehicle can relocate a large element. NASA's April 2026 architecture guide names “a marketplace for lunar logistics” as a goal but does not say what one would do. We study that question with a discrete-event testbed of surface cargo movement at a nine-site south-pole base, verified against thirteen analytic and published references (all within 1% of their reference, the M/G/1 queueing check within 0.5%) and cross-checked against an independent browser implementation on 24 metric cells. The testbed models lander arrivals and manifests, a heterogeneous fleet owned by several companies, the lunar night, Earth–Moon control latency fitted to NASA's teleoperation trials, vehicle faults, vehicles' other duties and the return flow of trash and samples. We compare four dispatch rules: bilateral in-house fleets (today's contracting), pooled greedy dispatch, batched optimal assignment, and a pooled marketplace built on a sequential single-item auction with bundling, consolidation of bulk lots, team lifts for oversize items, night-aware acceptance and anticipatory staging before published landings. Across about 190,000 simulated years, with 30 common-random-number replications per cell and sequential replication of the headline cells until the 99% confidence interval of the mean urgent delay is within 1% of the mean, we find four things. First, pooling matters only when the market is fragmented: with one owner the marketplace and in-house dispatch are statistically indistinguishable, but with six owners sharing six vehicles the in-house 90th-percentile delay of urgent cargo is {E3_GAIN} h longer than the marketplace's; across 45 fleet configurations the in-house delay follows a single curve in the effective number of vehicles per owner, 25 + 20/e hours (R² = 0.84). Second, any pooled rule meets a 90% same-day service level for urgent cargo with four vehicles at Phase-2 demand where in-house fleets do not reach it with eight; because the fleet is 99% of the cost, a surface move costs about $300 per kilogram of mass moved, or about $5,000 per kilogram of landed cargo, against a landed cost of $78,000–$1,000,000 per kilogram. Third, the heavy-cargo gap is a property of the manifest, not of dispatch: a capacity ladder derived from NASA's cargo classes shows that 1.6-tonne rovers can move 40% of Phase-2 landed mass, two of them lifting together 55%, a 10-tonne hauler 88%, and only a 15-tonne capability reaches 100%. Fourth, teleoperation from Earth is workable at lunar latency with a staffed operator pool and collapses at Mars-like delays, where delivered mass falls below 10%. We also report what did not help: anticipatory staging, priority credits and backhaul changed nothing measurable, and we explain why. We close with a reference design for how a lunar logistics marketplace should be built and operated, and release the testbed, scenarios, seeds, scripts and a browser version that reproduces the paper's cells.

Keywords: lunar surface logistics, multi-robot task allocation, auction mechanisms, discrete-event simulation, verification and validation, horizontal collaboration, Artemis, Moon Base

1. Introduction

A decade of lunar planning has settled where cargo will land and how much of it there will be. NASA's Moon Base Architecture User's Guide (April 2026) describes three phases: 21 landings delivering about 4,000 kg to the south pole in the first, 24 landings and about 60,000 kg in the second, 28 landings and about 150,000 kg in the third [1]. The agency's 2024 architecture white papers put recurring logistics at 2,000–6,000 kg per crewed mission, and occasional single elements such as habitats and pressurised rovers at 12,000–15,000 kg [2, 3]. Landing pads must sit more than a kilometre from the habitation zone because of plume ejecta, so everything that lands has to be moved, some of it as far as 5 km over slopes of up to 20 degrees [2].

Far less has been settled about who moves it. The two Lunar Terrain Vehicles (LTVs) ordered in May 2026, from Astrolab and Lunar Outpost at about $220 million each, are crew rovers that carry 800 kg of cargo at full performance and roughly 1,600 kg at reduced performance [4, 5]. NASA's 2025 logistics workshop states the mismatch plainly: “no mobility assets exist that can relocate large elements” [5]. Each vehicle will belong to one company under one contract, and NASA has told the LTV providers they may sell rover time to other customers between NASA missions [6]. In January 2025 NASA paid nine companies $24 million to study lunar logistics, including “surface cargo and mobility” and “business models” [7]. And on page 14 of the 2026 guide, the agency writes that “fostering a marketplace for lunar logistics will empower government and industry to develop the capabilities and operational competencies needed to support crewed missions to the Red Planet” [1].

That sentence is a goal, not a design. Nobody has said what a surface logistics marketplace would dispatch, with what rule, under which constraints, or whether it would help at all when the whole market is two rovers and a dozen cargo items a year. The question is not academic for us: Roatify is building a freight-dispatch platform for Earth trucking and intends to carry that software to the Moon in stages. Before raising money against that plan we wanted an honest answer to five questions.

  1. RQ1. When does pooled dispatch beat bilateral in-house fleets, and by how much, for fleets of two to eight vehicles split among one to eight owners?
  2. RQ2. How large is the heavy-cargo gap in delivered mass, how much of it can coordination (several rovers lifting one item together) recover, and what vehicle capacity closes it?
  3. RQ3. How do autonomy level and control latency bound surface throughput, and how many Earth operators does a fleet need?
  4. RQ4. What does a surface move cost per kilogram, how does it compare to the cost of landing that kilogram, and which assumptions drive it?
  5. RQ5. How should a lunar logistics marketplace be built and operated: which functions, which rules, which data, and in what order?

Earth freight gives reasons for both optimism and caution. Pooling fleets across shippers saves 5–30% of transport cost in road and forest logistics [8, 9, 10]. But those gains come from thick markets. Theory on dynamic matching says that in thin markets greedy matching is close to optimal and waiting to batch requests mostly adds delay [11, 12]; empirical work on US trucking ties the efficiency of the spot market directly to its thickness [13]. The lunar market will be the thinnest freight market anyone has ever tried to run.

We answer the five questions with simulation, because there is no data to answer them otherwise, and we hold the simulation to a standard that makes its numbers worth quoting. We built a discrete-event testbed of cargo movement at a nine-site south-pole base and ran about 8,000 simulated years of operations. The testbed reproduces NASA's published spacing, slopes, cargo masses, lander cadence and rover capacities; it adds the lunar night, Earth–Moon control latency that interpolates NASA's own teleoperation trial table exactly, vehicle faults, vehicles' other duties and a return flow of trash and samples. Thirteen verification checks compare it with analytic formulas, published measurements and conservation identities; an independent implementation in JavaScript reproduces its results within confidence intervals on every one of 24 metric cells; and the headline cells are replicated until the 99% confidence interval of the mean urgent delay is within 1% of the mean. On top of the testbed we implemented four dispatch rules, from today's bilateral contracting to a pooled marketplace that combines a sequential single-item auction with bundling, consolidation, team lifts, backhaul, night-aware acceptance and anticipatory staging. Our contributions are:

  • We introduce LunarSim, an open, verified discrete-event testbed for lunar surface logistics with three demand phases, three vehicle classes, a sourced parameter table, fourteen experiment suites and a browser version that runs the same model interactively. Each parameter carries its source and a confidence grade, and each verification check its reference and tolerance.
  • We propose a pooled dispatch marketplace for multi-owner rover fleets and give its clearing rule as a generalised-cost sequential auction (Algorithm 1), with team lift for items heavier than any single vehicle and anticipatory staging from the published landing schedule.
  • We show where pooling helps and where it does not, and give the result a form that transfers: the gain is zero for a single owner and grows with fragmentation and other duties, and the in-house 90th-percentile delay across 45 fleet configurations collapses onto one curve in the effective number of vehicles per owner. Any pooled rule reaches a same-day service level with four vehicles at Phase-2 demand; in-house fleets do not reach it with eight.
  • We quantify three limits that no dispatch rule removes, and give the first a general form: a capacity ladder that maps vehicle or team capacity to the share of NASA's manifest it can move; the collapse of teleoperation at Mars-like latency; and the fleet's fixed cost, which sets the price of a surface move regardless of how cleverly the fleet is dispatched.
  • We give a reference design for how a lunar logistics marketplace should be built and served: its functions, data, rules, governance and a phased rollout tied to NASA's Moon Base phases.

2. Background and related work

2.1 What NASA has published about surface cargo

Three NASA documents anchor our demand model. The Lunar Mobility Drivers and Needs white paper (2024) gives the range envelope (5–5,000 m), the ejecta standoff (>1,000 m), the slope envelope (up to 20°, with slopes over 10° “common”), the cargo classes (500–2,000 kg demonstrations, 2,000–6,000 kg recurring logistics per crewed mission, 12,000–15,000 kg habitation elements) and the capability expressed in the architecture (800 kg uncrewed on the LTV, about 1,500 kg planned in total) [2]. It lists the gaps the agency wants industry to fill: autonomy or tele-robotic operation through the whole year, standard cargo interfaces, offloading, levelling, mated power, and “the ability of multiple robotic mobility systems to work together” [2]. The Lunar Surface Cargo paper forecasts aggregate demand of 2,000–10,000 kg per year during the foundational phase, with occasional deliveries of up to 15,000 kg [3]. The 2026 architecture guide supplies the phase totals, a 10 km/h target for large rovers, a 2-tonne lander target for Phase 1, and the marketplace sentence quoted above [1].

2.2 Space logistics modelling

Campaign-level space logistics has a mature modelling literature. MIT's SpaceNet and the time-expanded network models of Ho, de Weck and colleagues optimise commodity flows between Earth, orbit and the lunar surface over mission campaigns [14, 15, 16]. These models decide how much mass to send and when; they treat the surface as a single node. The surface itself has been studied for traverse planning and rover energy budgeting (VIPER's planning deck gives speeds of 10–20 cm/s while driving and a mission average of 1 cm/s [17]), and for cooperative transport of large payloads at the control level [18, 19]. We found no model of dispatching cargo among several companies' vehicles on the surface, and no treatment of lander-arrival lumpiness, lighting windows and latency together.

2.3 Multi-robot task allocation and market-based coordination

Our mechanism sits in the market-based multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) tradition surveyed by Dias et al. [20] and formalised by Gerkey and Matarić [21]. The sequential single-item (SSI) auction of Koenig, Lagoudakis and colleagues allocates tasks one at a time to the robot whose route cost increases least; its total path cost is within a factor of two of optimal under symmetric, triangle-inequality costs, and it needs at most |tasks|×|robots| bids [22, 23]. The consensus-based bundle algorithm (CBBA) decentralises the same idea [24]. These methods assume one cooperative owner and have no notion of price or of items that need two robots. We keep the SSI structure, replace route cost with a generalised cost that includes the shipper's value of time, and add a team-lift stage.

2.4 Freight marketplaces, collaboration and market thickness

Combinatorial auctions for truckload procurement [25], horizontal collaboration among carriers [8, 9] and digital freight matching [26] all report that pooling demand across shippers cuts cost, typically by 5–30%, and that the hard part is a fair split of the gain [9, 10]. Dynamic matching theory adds the thin-market caveat: without information about which requests are about to expire, greedy matching is near-optimal and batching mostly adds waiting [11]; a windowed matching that waits d+1 periods is only 0.279-competitive [12]. On US spot trucking, Harris and Nguyen find efficiency rising with thickness [13]. Even in that market only about 15% of loads clear on the spot exchange; the rest move under contracts [27]. In dynamic vehicle routing, exploiting known future demand to reposition vehicles is a classic lever [58]; the lunar version of that lever is the published landing schedule, which we test in Section 6.11.

2.5 Teleoperation under delay and supervisory control

Round-trip light time to the Moon is about 2.6 s; with relays and ground processing NASA quotes up to 10 s one way [28]. NASA JSC's LTV teleoperation trials measured average driving speeds of 3.24, 2.56, 2.03 and 1.76 km/h at 0, 4, 6 and 8 s of round-trip delay [29]. A lunar-excavation teleoperation study found task completion times of 139% and 174% of baseline at 1.5 s and 3 s delay, with success rates falling from 91% to 45% [30]. Operators adopt a move-and-wait strategy once delay exceeds about a second [31]. For supervisory control, Olsen and Wood's fan-out metric gives 1–9 robots per operator depending on autonomy [32], and mining autonomous haulage runs up to about 30 trucks per controller [33]. We interpolate the JSC table exactly in Section 3.4 and sweep operator fan-out in Section 6.5.

2.6 Simulation verification and reporting

Our verification programme follows the standard texts: analytic queueing references for the engine core, common random numbers with paired confidence intervals, and sequential replication until a target precision is reached [43, 59]. Reporting follows the ODD protocol for agent-based models [34] and the STRESS guidelines for discrete-event simulation [35]; the cross-implementation check is in the spirit of the reproducibility checklists now common in machine-learning venues [57].
